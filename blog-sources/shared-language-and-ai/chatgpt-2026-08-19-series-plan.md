## Central thesis

> **Communities become ecosystems—and ecosystems become marketplaces—when participants develop a shared language for describing what they are creating, exchanging, and evaluating.**

Pantone is valuable not because it “owns colors,” but because it created a trusted coordination protocol. A designer, printer, manufacturer, and customer can refer to the same identifier and expect approximately the same physical result. The product is not really the swatch; it is the reduction of ambiguity.

That also makes Pantone a useful example of the tension between **proprietary standards and open standards**. When Adobe phased Pantone libraries out of its applications beginning in 2022, designers did not lose the ability to make similar-looking colors. They lost convenient access to an established shared vocabulary embedded in their workflow. Adobe’s final remaining Pantone books were removed from Illustrator in October 2023. citeturn533661search0turn533661search18

## The important distinction

You should avoid presenting every free color project as an equivalent replacement for Pantone.

There are several different layers:

- **Pantone:** a commercial color-matching and production ecosystem connecting digital references, physical swatches, inks, substrates, printers, and manufacturers.
- **FREETONE:** a free palette containing 1,280 “Pantone-ish” colors, created partly as a protest against restricted access. It is an alternative vocabulary inside software, but not a comparable global physical-production network. citeturn533661search3turn533661news62
- **Open Color:** an MIT-licensed palette optimized primarily for user interfaces. It is genuinely open, but it solves a narrower problem than Pantone. citeturn533661search8
- **CSS Color 4 and OKLCH:** open technical standards for representing and manipulating colors across software and the web. citeturn533661search5
- **Design Tokens:** a machine-readable shared language for exchanging colors, typography, spacing, and other design decisions between tools. The W3C community group published its first stable specification in October 2025, emphasizing theming, modern color spaces, and interoperability. citeturn448836search0turn448836search4

That difference actually improves the argument:

> An open list of values is not enough. A successful standard also requires governance, tooling, adoption, trust, verification, and participation across the entire supply chain.

## Article 1: Pantone Is a Protocol

Possible title:

### **Pantone Isn’t a Color Company. It’s a Coordination Protocol.**

The article could begin with a deceptively simple question:

> What color is red?

A person can answer emotionally or descriptively: crimson, brick, blood red, warm red, faded red. But none of those descriptions is sufficient when someone must manufacture 20,000 packages in three countries.

Pantone converts a subjective property into a portable reference. It gives the community:

- A vocabulary
- A numbering system
- Physical reference objects
- Expectations about reproduction
- A dispute-resolution mechanism
- An ecosystem of compatible vendors

The larger argument is that standards function like languages. The individual symbols are not especially useful by themselves. Their value comes from everyone agreeing to interpret them similarly.

The Adobe dispute then becomes the tension at the center of the piece:

> What happens when a community’s shared language is controlled by a private company that can change the terms of access?

The problem is not simply paying for colors. It is that a foundational coordination layer can become a toll road.

## Article 2: Communities Need Languages Before They Can Become Markets

Possible title:

### **Before You Can Build a Marketplace, You Need a Dictionary**

A marketplace cannot scale when every transaction requires participants to negotiate the meaning of the product from scratch.

Shared languages make several things possible:

1. **Discovery:** Buyers can search for a known category.
2. **Comparison:** Products can be evaluated using common attributes.
3. **Compatibility:** Third parties can build complementary tools.
4. **Reputation:** Participants can agree on what quality means.
5. **Automation:** Software can process structured descriptions.
6. **Specialization:** Participants can operate on one part of the system without understanding every other part.

You can move outward from color into examples like:

- File formats
- Programming-language package interfaces
- Electrical connectors
- Shipping-container dimensions
- Music notation and MIDI
- Web protocols
- Design tokens
- Card-game terminology
- RPG statistics and dice notation
- Product taxonomies in marketplaces

The pattern is:

> **Vocabulary → coordination → interoperability → specialization → ecosystem → marketplace**

Open standards are particularly powerful because participants can build against them without seeking individual permission. But openness alone does not guarantee adoption. A technically excellent language nobody speaks is less economically useful than an imperfect standard embedded throughout an industry.

That gives you a nuanced position rather than simply “open good, proprietary bad.” Proprietary standards can bootstrap trust and adoption. The danger arrives when the ecosystem becomes dependent on them without meaningful portability or shared governance.

## Article 3: AI May Make Shared Languages Invisible

Possible title:

### **AI Could Eliminate the Shared Language—and Make It More Important Than Ever**

This is the most interesting part of the idea.

Historically, humans needed to learn the standard directly. You needed to know a Pantone number, a database schema, a query language, or the terminology used by a particular marketplace.

AI can become a translation layer:

> “Give me the green used on old military field equipment, but slightly darker and suitable for printing on recycled cardboard.”

An AI system could translate that fuzzy human request into:

- A perceptual color value
- A print-process recommendation
- A manufacturer-specific formulation
- A design token
- An accessibility-safe digital variant
- Several fallback formats

The person no longer needs to speak the formal language.

But the formal language has not disappeared. It has moved underneath the interface.

### The paradox

> **AI may reduce the need for humans to learn standards while increasing the need for machines to have precise, open, interoperable standards.**

An AI agent cannot reliably transact with manufacturers, software tools, and other agents using vibes alone. Somewhere in the process, “dark military green” must become structured data with explicit semantics.

The future may therefore have two layers:

- **Human layer:** informal, contextual, personal and multilingual
- **Machine layer:** highly structured identifiers, schemas, ontologies and conversion rules

AI handles the translation between them.

## AI as a language-forming participant

The speculative extension is that AI will not merely translate existing languages. It could participate in creating new ones.

Multi-agent research already studies systems in which agents develop communication protocols optimized for their shared tasks. Experiments have produced behavior resembling convergence on majority languages, creole-like protocols and varying degrees of mutual intelligibility between neighboring agent communities. citeturn448836academia41turn448836academia43

That creates several plausible futures.

### 1. AI accelerates linguistic evolution

New ideas could acquire names almost immediately because millions of people are asking models to describe similar experiences. The model recognizes the recurring unnamed concept and begins suggesting a concise term.

Today, language generally evolves through distributed cultural repetition. AI could dramatically shorten that loop.

### 2. Models create de facto standard vocabularies

When enough people use the same assistants, the model’s preferred terminology becomes the default language of documentation, business communication, product categories and even emotional expression.

The standard may never be formally approved. It becomes standard because the model repeatedly suggests it.

### 3. AI homogenizes language

Research has found that AI assistance can improve individual creative output while reducing diversity across the collection of outputs. Other controlled writing studies have found increased similarity and reduced lexical or content diversity among AI-assisted writing. citeturn386242search0turn386242academia31

There is also evidence that writing suggestions from Western-centric models can shift non-Western users toward Western writing norms, affecting not only content but style. citeturn386242search7turn386242search19

The risk is not simply that writing becomes bland. It is that:

> The model becomes an invisible standards body for culture.

### 4. AI creates fragmented machine dialects

The opposite is also possible. Different model families, companies, communities and agent networks could develop specialized internal terminology.

Humans might encounter:

- Company-specific AI dialects
- Profession-specific compressed vocabularies
- Agent-to-agent protocols humans rarely inspect
- New words that originate in synthetic communication
- Terms whose precise meaning exists primarily in embedding space rather than dictionary definitions

Instead of one homogenized language, we could get countless machine-assisted dialects—with AI translators making the fragmentation mostly invisible.

## The strongest unifying argument

The series should not ultimately be about color.

It should be about this:

> **A shared language is infrastructure. It determines who can participate, who can build compatible products, who can mediate transactions, and who gets to define what things mean.**

Pantone makes that visible because color feels universal and natural. Yet converting “the same blue” into a reliable industrial outcome requires standards, physical references, tools, governance and trust.

AI then raises the stakes. When software increasingly mediates how people describe the world, standards are no longer just technical agreements. They shape culture.
