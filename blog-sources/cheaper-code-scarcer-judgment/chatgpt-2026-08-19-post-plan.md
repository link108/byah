## Assistant

This is a strong blog idea because **Jevons paradox gives you a clean explanation for why AI is not simply “replacing software work.”** As the cost of producing software falls, people attempt far more software—more features, prototypes, internal tools, experiments, and niche products. The scarce resource moves elsewhere.

## Core thesis

> AI makes building software dramatically cheaper, but that does not make judgment less valuable. It increases the amount of software we attempt to create, which makes product judgment, domain knowledge, UX, quality systems, and the ability to identify worthwhile problems more important than ever.

The modern software bottleneck is shifting from:

> “Can we build this?”

to:

> “Should we build this, what exactly should it do, and how do we make it trustworthy?”

## How Jevons paradox applies

Jevons paradox originally describes how increasing the efficiency of using a resource can increase total consumption of that resource rather than reduce it.

Applied to AI-assisted development:

- The cost of implementing an idea falls.
- More people can produce software.
- Existing developers can attempt more ideas and larger scopes.
- Companies expect greater feature velocity.
- More code, interfaces, automations, and AI-generated decisions enter production.
- The total demand for software development—and for everything surrounding it—may grow.

AI does not necessarily mean “the same amount of software with fewer engineers.” It may mean **ten times as much software, produced by similar-sized teams, with much greater pressure on product direction and operational quality.**

That connects naturally to your other blog series about AI increasing the need for DevOps, QA, observability, and automation.

## Your personal angle

The essay could start with your experience of AI changing what you spend time on.

You can now move from an idea to a working implementation much faster. But that has not eliminated the difficult parts of your work. Instead, it has exposed them:

- Deciding what the product should actually do
- Giving an agent enough context to make good choices
- Detecting when plausible-looking output is wrong
- Designing workflows that users can understand and trust
- Building tests, deployment systems, metrics, and feedback loops
- Knowing when a generated feature is technically functional but conceptually misguided

You could describe the paradox at an individual level:

> AI reduced the cost of trying an idea, so I started trying more ideas. That did not leave me with less work. It left me with more products to evaluate, more design decisions to make, more generated code to validate, and more opportunities to build the wrong thing faster.

That sentence could anchor the piece.

## The non-tech-interest argument

This is probably the most distinctive part of the article.

When implementation becomes abundant, **the source of valuable ideas increasingly comes from outside implementation itself.**

Someone with intimate knowledge of backpacking, tea, aquariums, tabletop games, logistics, medicine, construction, music, or any other domain can recognize problems that a technically skilled outsider will miss. They understand:

- Which frustrations are real versus merely theoretical
- What users already do to work around them
- Which constraints cannot be ignored
- What would feel natural inside the existing culture
- What people would actually trust or pay for
- Which “obvious” technical solutions would fail in practice

AI can provide broad knowledge, but broad knowledge is different from lived familiarity. It may know the vocabulary of a hobby without knowing what participants care about, which compromises they tolerate, or which tiny recurring annoyance is worth solving.

A useful framing:

> In the past, being deeply technical gave you access to a scarce means of production. In an AI-rich world, understanding a worthwhile problem may become scarcer than the ability to produce a solution.

That makes being well-rounded economically valuable, not merely personally enriching.

## A useful nuance

I would avoid arguing that coding knowledge no longer matters. A stronger claim is that its role changes.

Technical expertise helps you:

- Recognize generated nonsense
- Understand architectural consequences
- Control complexity
- Design appropriate tests and guardrails
- Know what should not be delegated
- Turn a prototype into a dependable system

So the winning combination is not “domain expert instead of engineer.” It is:

> **Technical leverage + domain intimacy + product judgment + systems for quality.**

That also makes the essay more grounded and less like a generic “AI means ideas are everything” argument. Ideas alone are cheap too. The valuable capability is recognizing a meaningful problem, forming a coherent product opinion, and repeatedly turning it into something dependable.
